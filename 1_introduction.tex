
\section{Introduction}

Inertial measurement units (IMUs) are among the most widely deployed sensors in consumer
electronics. Every smartphone and smartwatch carries one, and the applications that would
follow from reading them well are not exotic: confirming that a patient actually performed
the prescribed rehabilitation exercises~\cite{pamap2}, flagging the repeated overhead reach
that will injure a warehouse worker, telling a physiotherapist which of two similar-looking
squats was the bad one, or scoring sleep and posture over a week of ordinary
life~\cite{chan2024capture24}. What these share is that the activity of interest is defined
\emph{by the deployment}, not by a benchmark: it is a specific exercise for a specific
patient, or a task vocabulary particular to one workplace. Yet nearly every such application
is built from scratch---collect a bespoke dataset, hire annotators, train a dataset-specific
classifier, and hard-code a fixed sensor layout and a fixed label set. Human activity
recognition (HAR) still has no foundation model a developer can simply pick up: one
pretrained model that ingests whatever sensors a device exposes, recognizes the
application's own activity names, and asks for as little labelled target data as the
deployment can afford.

The field's dominant answer has been scale. We argue that scale alone cannot deliver this,
for two reasons that are specific to inertial sensing, and that these two reasons dictate
two design choices rather than a bigger corpus.

\textbf{(L1) IMU admits no closed grammar.}
Language and vision foundation models work because their substrate is, in a real sense,
covered by a large corpus: text is generated by a grammar and a finite lexicon, and natural
images obey a stable projective geometry, so enough data does approach the space of possible
inputs. Inertial signals have no comparable closure. The same motion, recorded from the same
body, becomes a different signal if the device is rotated, worn on a different limb,
sampled at a different rate, low-pass filtered by an unknown firmware stage, or reported as
gravity-inclusive total acceleration rather than the gravity-removed convention of a
different operating system. These are not rare corner cases; they are what changes when an
app ships on a second phone model. A larger corpus reduces the \emph{probability} of
surprise, but it cannot close the space, because the transformations are continuous and
compose. Compatibility with an unseen acquisition configuration therefore has to be built in
as \emph{inductive bias}, not learned from coverage.

Inductive bias, though, is a quantity to be \emph{calibrated}, not maximised. Assume too
much---a fixed channel layout, a fixed sampling rate---and the encoder cannot read a device
it was not built for. Assume too little---treat every scalar channel as an independent
series carrying only a free-text name---and nothing prevents the model from using that text
as a dataset identifier, since in a multi-corpus training set the description of a channel is
very nearly a label for which corpus it came from. HALO's tokenizer occupies the middle: it
commits to the physics that is genuinely universal (co-located axes form a rigid sensor;
motion energy lives at physical frequencies; gravity is a signed DC offset) and refuses to
commit to anything that varies by deployment (rate, channel count, ordering, placement,
which sensors exist at all).

\textbf{(L2) Sensor similarity does not track linguistic similarity.}
The standard remedy for open-ended label sets is to project sensor features into a sentence
embedding space and retrieve the nearest label
string~\cite{clip,lanhar,goat}. This presumes that the two geometries agree. They do not, and
they disagree in both directions. \emph{Drinking coffee} and \emph{drinking water} are the
same wrist trajectory but only $0.56$ apart in text; \emph{sitting} and \emph{sitting
down}---a static posture and a dynamic transition, about as different as two IMU windows
get---sit at $0.93$. Across a set of $14$ label pairs we selected for this contrast and
measured with the same frozen sentence encoder the field uses (Section~\ref{sec:geometry}),
$38$ of the $49$ cross-comparisons are \emph{inverted}: a pair of physically distinct
activities is scored more similar than a pair of physically identical ones. A single
projection asked to reconcile these two geometries is mis-specified, and the easiest way for
it to reduce training loss is to exploit whatever \emph{does} predict the label within a
corpus---subject, session, device---which is precisely the dataset memorisation that
cross-dataset evaluation is meant to catch.

\textbf{HALO.} We present HALO (\textbf{H}eterogeneity-\textbf{A}ware
\textbf{L}anguage-conditioned \textbf{O}pen-set model), which answers each obstacle with a design
choice. Against L1, a tokenizer with calibrated inductive bias: co-located axes are grouped
into \emph{sensors}, each read on a fixed physical-frequency axis by a constant-$Q$
filterbank computed from a native-rate transform, so the representation is rate-invariant and
anti-aliased \emph{by construction}---no resampling exists anywhere in the path---while
per-token observability masks state which frequency bands a given rate and window could
actually measure rather than fabricating them. Sensor identity (device, modality, placement,
gravity convention) enters as natural language attached to the \emph{sensor}, not to each
scalar channel, so channel count and ordering are free while the conditioning stays too
coarse to serve as a corpus fingerprint. Against L2, an \emph{evidence engine} that declines
to reconcile the two geometries at all: rather than projecting sensor features into label
space, it retrieves encoded exemplars from a memory by \emph{sensor} similarity and
accumulates evidence for whichever candidate label strings the deployment declares at run
time. Representation pretraining is entirely activity-label-free; labels enter only as
annotations on retrieved memory entries.

\textbf{(L3) The quantity that matters is label efficiency.}
These two choices are usually argued about under the banner of ``zero-shot versus
fine-tuning,'' which we think is a false dichotomy and a bad way to measure the thing. A
deployment does not choose between zero labels and a full annotation campaign; it has a
budget, and the question is what that budget buys. HALO is therefore evaluated on a single
\emph{label-efficiency curve}: the same frozen model recognises declared labels with no
target data at all, improves as a handful of demonstrated examples are appended to memory
with no gradient update and nothing leaving the device, and remains available for
conventional supervised fitting when labels are plentiful. Zero-shot is the left endpoint of
that curve, not a separate claim.

We train on 12 public HAR datasets spanning 20 sensor streams and 93 activity labels, and
evaluate on 7 held-out datasets under a leakage-free, subject-disjoint protocol that scores
against each dataset's own label strings.

We make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We argue that inertial sensing has \emph{no closed grammar}, so cross-configuration
  compatibility must come from calibrated inductive bias rather than from corpus coverage,
  and we realise that bias as a tokenizer that is rate-invariant and anti-aliased by
  construction, groups co-located axes into rigid sensors, and conditions on
  \emph{per-sensor} rather than per-channel language---coarse enough to describe an unseen
  device, too coarse to memorise which corpus a window came from.
  \item We show, by direct measurement on our own $93$-label vocabulary, that sensor
  similarity and linguistic similarity are not merely imperfectly correlated but frequently
  \emph{inverted}, and we draw the architectural consequence: HALO retrieves in sensor space
  and votes in label space through an \emph{evidence engine}, so the two geometries are never
  forced through one projection. Representation pretraining uses no activity labels at all.
  \item We reframe the evaluation as a single \emph{label-efficiency curve} spanning $k{=}0$
  declared-label-only recognition, $k$ demonstrated exemplars appended to memory without any
  gradient update, and full supervision---and we establish a leakage-free, subject-disjoint
  cross-dataset protocol that scores every model against each target dataset's own label
  strings, with a dataset-identity probe that tests directly whether an encoder's
  conditioning has degenerated into corpus memorisation.
  \item We package the model behind a small \texttt{predict()} interface and demonstrate it
  in an on-device iOS application. We will release the pretrained checkpoint and code as a
  reusable foundation for mobile HAR.
\end{itemize}
